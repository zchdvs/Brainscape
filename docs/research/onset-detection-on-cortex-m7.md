## Summary

- **The trigger layer is buildable inside the remaining CPU budget by a wide margin, for either candidate architecture.** A CMSIS-DSP spectral-flux detector at a real-time-appropriate window/hop (aubio's own shipped defaults: 512-sample window, 256-sample hop at 44.1/48 kHz) costs on the order of **~20,000–35,000 cycles per analysis frame, amortizing to roughly 80–140 cycles per audio sample** — about 0.8–1.4% of the Daisy's 10,000-cycle/sample budget, derived from ARM's own published FFT cost model, not the ~80–120-cycle placeholder the sibling budget document flagged as needing real research. A multi-band time-domain filterbank detector is cheaper still, at roughly **150–300 cycles/sample for 6–8 stereo bands (~1.5–3%)**. **CPU was never the real constraint here — RAM, latency, and algorithmic reliability are.**
- **CMSIS-DSP's FFT twiddle-factor tables are compile-time `const` arrays, not runtime-allocated RAM** — verified directly from `arm_rfft_fast_init_f32.c` and the `arm_rfft_fast_instance_f32`/`arm_cfft_instance_f32` struct definitions in `CMSIS-DSP/Include/dsp/transform_functions.h`. Every field is a scalar or a pointer into `const` (flash-resident) tables; the instance struct itself is on the order of 32–64 bytes. **The task brief's framing — "RAM for twiddle tables and buffers" — bundles two very different costs: twiddle tables cost flash, not RAM, and are effectively free on a platform with 128 KB–8 MB of flash.** The real RAM cost is the input/output/scratch/history buffers, which this document sizes explicitly (§3.2): on the order of **6–10 KiB** for a 512-point real-FFT spectral-flux detector, comfortably inside the Daisy's internal SRAM/DTCM.
- **No single published onset-detection method reliably solves both of Brainscape's named failure cases at once, and the literature says so explicitly** — this is the central, honest finding of this document. Bello et al.'s 2005 tutorial (the field's standard reference) states plainly that magnitude-only methods (energy, HFC, spectral flux) "neglect the detection of events without a strong energy increase: e.g., low notes, transitions between harmonically related notes, **or onsets played by bowed instruments**" — precisely Brainscape's slow-attack-pad case — while phase-based and complex-domain methods, designed to compensate, are themselves degraded by "**phase distortions common to complex commercial music recordings (e.g., audio effects)**" — precisely Brainscape's heavily-distorted-guitar case. [Bello et al. 2005](https://hajim.rochester.edu/ece/sites/zduan/teaching/ece472/reading/Bello_2005.pdf)
- **Distortion's specific failure mode for spectral-flux-family detectors is documented, not hypothetical: performance drops "when comparing successive notes sharing a considerable number of harmonics."** Heavy clipping is a broadband harmonic generator — it makes *every* note's spectrum harmonically dense and mutually similar, which is exactly the condition under which magnitude-difference-based detection functions (energy, HFC, spectral flux, and by extension a magnitude-only multi-band filterbank) lose their discriminating signal. This is a structural limitation, not a tuning problem — no amount of threshold adjustment fixes a detection function that is looking at a spectral quantity which genuinely stopped changing between notes. [Springer/Musicological guitar onset paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13636-021-00214-7) (via search-indexed abstract, full text access-gated — see Open Questions).
- **The complex-domain / phase-deviation family exists specifically to address the "repeated notes, same pitch, insufficient magnitude change" case** — the closest documented analogue to palm-muted, same-root-note distorted-guitar chugging. The mechanism: a freshly-plucked string (even under identical distortion, identical pitch, identical loudness) resets the oscillator's phase, and "**the phase deviation function makes use of the fact that frequency components of the new notes are unlikely to be in phase with the previous note**" — a cue that survives amplitude clipping (a static, memoryless nonlinearity) far better than it survives spectral-magnitude comparison. This is the single most on-point piece of prior art for Brainscape's stated distorted-guitar target case, at the cost of being *itself* degraded by heavy post-processing/effects distortion of the phase spectrum per the Bello finding above — the two effects partially cancel, which is why Dixon's large-scale 2006 re-test found spectral flux, complex-domain, and weighted-phase-deviation "all achieve a similarly high level of performance," with **no clear winner** and spectral flux retaining "the advantage of being the simplest and fastest algorithm." [Bello/Duxbury/Davies/Sandler, IEEE SPL 2004](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/3343056_On_the_Use_of_Phase_and_Energy_for_Musical_Onset_Detection_in_the_Complex_Domain); [Dixon, DAFx 2006](https://www.ofai.at/papers/oefai-tr-2006-12.pdf).
- **A cheap multi-band time-domain envelope-follower filterbank buys real robustness over a single broadband envelope follower, but it inherits spectral flux's exact harmonic-overlap blind spot — it does not buy what phase/complex-domain methods buy.** A multi-band filterbank is still a *magnitude*-only technique (energy per band, not phase per band); splitting the spectrum into 6–8 bands lets a note change register even when the *broadband* envelope is flat (e.g., a new note's energy shifts from one band to another with total loudness unchanged — a real, common case with pickup EQ curves and distortion's uneven harmonic spread) — but a genuinely same-pitch repeated note under heavy clipping, which the brief specifically flags, changes essentially none of the per-band energies either, and a magnitude-only filterbank has no phase channel to fall back on. **This is a materially different, more honest answer than "multiband gets you most of the way there" — it gets you real, cheap robustness against one common near-miss (energy relocating between bands) but not against the literal case the brief names as Brainscape's marketing claim (same-note distorted chugging).**
- **A genuine, permissively-licensed, decades-shipped reference implementation of exactly this "multi-band filterbank + per-band adaptive threshold" architecture already exists: Pure Data's `bonk~` (Miller Puckette, BSD-3-Clause).** Verified from source: 11 bands by default (`DEFNFILTERS 11`), each band's power tracked via a sliding bounded-Q filter, with a **per-band decaying "mask"** (`maskpow *= maskdecay`, default decay 0.7 every `masktime`-frames) acting as the adaptive floor, and an onset declared via **growth hysteresis** — current power crossing `hithresh` (default 5×) above the masked baseline to start, falling below `lothresh` (default 2.5×) to reset — a real, field-tested, dependency-free algorithm Brainscape can study or directly adapt under a permissive license, unlike aubio or Essentia (below). [bonk~.c source](https://github.com/pure-data/pure-data/blob/master/extra/bonk~/bonk~.c); [Puckette/Apel/Zicarelli, ICMC 1998](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265414446_Real-time_audio_analysis_tools_for_Pd_and_MSP).
- **aubio (GPLv3) and Essentia (AGPLv3, dual-licensed with a paid commercial tier) are both study-only for a permissively- or even GPLv3-licensed Brainscape core that isn't itself willing to adopt AGPL's network-copyleft terms** — but both are excellent, directly-relevant reading: aubio ships nine detection functions (energy, hfc, complex, phase, wphase, specdiff, kl, mkl, specflux) behind one API and a moving-median adaptive-threshold peak-picker with sane, battle-tested real-time defaults (512-window/256-hop, threshold 0.3, silence gate −90 dB, 20 ms minimum inter-onset interval); Essentia ships a near-identical six-method set (hfc, complex, complex_phase, flux, melflux, rms) plus a dedicated Mel-band-limited flux variant. Both confirm the field's converged-on default method set and parameter ranges, which Brainscape can reimplement from the published algorithms (algorithms are not copyrightable; only the specific code is) without any licensing entanglement. [aubio](https://aubio.org/), [aubio COPYING (GPLv3)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aubio/aubio/master/COPYING), [Essentia `onsetdetection.cpp`](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/master/src/algorithms/rhythm/onsetdetection.cpp), [Essentia license](https://essentia.upf.edu/).
- **Adaptive whitening (Stowell & Plumbley 2007), aubio's own built-in feature, is the standard technique for making one fixed threshold work across wildly different input levels and instruments (single-coil, fuzz pedal, line-level synth) without user tuning of the threshold itself** — it normalizes each spectral bin against a slowly-decaying per-bin peak memory before the detection function is computed, so the *shape* of the detection function becomes largely level-independent. This is a cheap post-processing step (one compare + one multiply per bin per frame) applicable equally to a spectral-flux or a multi-band time-domain design, and is a stronger, better-documented mechanism for "no user tuning across wildly different sources" than threshold adjustment alone. [Stowell & Plumbley, ICMC 2007 (title/abstract verified; full text access issues — see Open Questions)]; [aubio onset docs, adaptive-whitening parameter](https://aubio.org/manual/latest/py_spectral.html).
- **Hologram's own Chroma Console already ships exactly the calibration routine the brief asks Brainscape to design, and it is genuinely simple: enter a calibration menu, play your instrument normally for a few seconds (a strummed chord for guitar, a few notes for synth/line-level), and the pedal auto-selects one of four input headroom levels** (Low/Medium/High/Very High), which it remembers across power cycles and applies to every preset — with a manual four-button override available. This is a real, shipped, one-gesture UX precedent Brainscape can copy almost directly, extended (per this document's recommendation) to also calibrate the onset detector's noise floor and threshold, not just input headroom/gain. [Hologram Chroma Console manual](https://www.hologramelectronics.com/pages/chroma-console-manual), corroborated via [ManualsLib](https://www.manualslib.com/guide/4257002/hologram-chroma-console-multi-effect-pedal-user-manual.html).
- **A minimum inherent latency is unavoidable with any window-based spectral method, and it is a real, non-trivial addition on top of the Microcosm's already-criticized onset-to-effect delay.** At aubio's real-time defaults (512-window/256-hop @ 48 kHz), the detector cannot produce its first output before the window has filled: **~10.7 ms** minimum, plus the hop-quantization and any look-ahead in the peak-picker (Dixon's formula looks `w=3` frames, ~1.7 ms at this hop, into the future for a true local maximum) — call it a **realistic 10–20 ms floor for onset-to-decision latency**, before the grain scheduler even acts on it. A time-domain multi-band envelope-follower detector's latency is instead set by its smoothing time constant, which can be pushed well under 5 ms (even under 1 ms for a fast-attack design) at the cost of more false triggers from noise — a genuinely different, more favorable latency/robustness trade for a device whose own prior-art competitor is criticized for feeling slow to trigger.
- **DaisySP ships no onset-detection, envelope-follower, or gate primitive of any kind** — verified by absence from `daisysp.h`'s module list (confirmed via the same audit method sibling documents used for `GranularPlayer` and `Looper`). The closest module, `adsr.h`, is an *output* envelope generator, not an *input* envelope follower. **Brainscape must write this layer from scratch either way**, which is actually good news for the multi-band-vs-spectral decision: neither path is "free" from an existing DaisySP class, so the choice should be made on the merits in §2–§4, not on implementation convenience.
- **Real-world precedent for the "no user tuning across wildly different sources" problem already exists outside the granular-pedal world and offers useful vocabulary and mechanisms**: electronic drum trigger modules (Roland, Alesis) universally expose **Threshold** (minimum level to register a hit), **Sensitivity** (velocity-to-loudness curve), **Crosstalk/Mask** (suppress a false trigger induced by a neighboring transducer), and **Retrigger Cancel** (suppress a double-trigger from one physical hit's decaying ring) as four independent, well-understood knobs — a more complete and more honestly-named control set than "sensitivity" alone, and directly reusable as Brainscape's trigger-parameter vocabulary. Roland's own hex-pickup guitar synths (GR-55/GK-3) additionally require **per-string** sensitivity calibration, a concrete precedent for calibrating a trigger detector per input characteristic rather than once globally. [digitalDrummer, "Understanding trigger parameters"](https://digitaldrummermag.com/2023/08/10/understanding-trigger-parameters/); [Alesis DM10 manual](https://www.alesis.com/rscdn/908/documents/dm10_module___operator__s_manual___revc.pdf); [Roland GR-55 pickup sensitivity support article](https://support.roland.com/hc/en-us/articles/201919469-GR-55-Setting-the-Pickup-Sensitivity).
- **Selectable trigger source as a first-class, per-mode parameter (onset / clock / stochastic / MIDI note / footswitch / external sidechain audio) is cheap to implement and has direct hardware precedent in the wider synth/eurorack world** for the sidechain case specifically — Eurorack input/envelope-follower-plus-comparator modules (Doepfer A-119, Mutable Instruments Ears) exist precisely to turn an arbitrary audio signal into a gate, which is the same operation a "sidechain trigger" mode needs; layering this onto Brainscape's own onset detector's output stage (comparator + hysteresis on whatever detection function is chosen) costs nothing beyond an input-source mux already justified by microcosm.md §13.3's guaranteed-working-fallback recommendation. [Doepfer A-119](https://www.sweetwater.com/store/detail/A-119-VE--doepfer-a-119-external-input-envelope-follower-eurorack-module-vintage-edition); [FTelettronica Envelope Follower/Triggered Filter](https://www.ftelettronica.com/product/envelope-follower-triggered-filter/).

## 1. Why onset detection is hard for this input class

Brainscape's trigger layer must work across a wider dynamic and timbral range than almost any onset-detection literature target, because the input is genuinely unconstrained: a passive single-coil guitar (low output, high source impedance, strong pick transient), a fuzz/distortion pedal (high output, heavily compressed/clipped, weak or absent transient), and a line-level synth pad (any output level, frequently a slow, deliberately non-transient attack). The two failure cases the brief names map onto two structurally different problems, and it matters that they are different, because a single fix does not address both:

1. **"There is no transient to find" — the slow-attack pad / bowed-string case.** A synth pad with a multi-second attack envelope, or a bowed string, genuinely has no fast energy rise anywhere in the signal at note onset by the performer's own design. No signal-processing cleverness invents information that was never encoded in the waveform. The only available cues are *indirect*: a change in pitch, a change in harmonic content, or (if the instrument is polyphonic/gestural, e.g., MIDI or a hex pickup) an out-of-band control signal. This is why Bello et al. explicitly reach for phase-based methods in this case — not because phase-based methods find a bigger transient, but because a new pitch produces a detectable phase discontinuity even when the amplitude envelope is smooth by design. [Bello et al. 2005, §III-A.3 / §V-C]
2. **"The transient is there, but it's buried under a constantly-high noise floor" — the heavily distorted/compressed guitar case.** A fuzz or heavy distortion circuit is, functionally, a fast limiter: it compresses the guitar's natural pick-to-sustain dynamic range down to a few dB, so the *broadband envelope* barely moves from note to note (the classic Microcosm complaint: "Microcosm is simply silent and adds nothing to the dry signal" under heavy distortion, per `microcosm.md` §12.1). But the transient is not actually gone from the underlying waveform's fine structure — it is present as (a) a genuine, if small, brief overshoot before the clipper's feedback/compression settles, (b) a burst of new harmonic content as a differently-voiced note is clipped differently, and (c) a phase discontinuity at the string's fresh excitation, which clipping — a static, memoryless, sample-by-sample nonlinearity — does not erase. The problem for spectral-magnitude methods specifically is that clipping *also* generates so much broadband harmonic energy on every note that successive notes' spectra become mutually similar, directly undermining magnitude-difference detection (§2.3).

A third, related failure mode worth naming explicitly because it is common on guitar and is neither of the above: **repeated notes at the same pitch with a short, percussive articulation** (palm-muted chugging, a defining texture of heavy-distortion playing) changes essentially nothing about pitch, timbre class, or (under heavy compression) loudness between hits — only the phase/timing resets. This is the case the complex-domain/phase-deviation literature was built for (§2.4), and it is worth Brainscape's design explicitly targeting it by name, since it is likely the single most common "the pedal went silent" complaint scenario for a metal/hard-rock player.

**Net framing for the rest of this document:** Brainscape needs (a) a detection function honestly evaluated against both failure cases rather than marketed as solving both, (b) a cost model proving whichever choice is made fits the budget trivially, (c) an adaptive-threshold/calibration layer that removes the need for the player to understand any of this, and (d) a guaranteed-working non-audio fallback (clock, MIDI note, footswitch) for the inevitable cases no audio-domain method will ever catch — a fifth-generation continuation of what the Microcosm's community has been asking for since 2020 (`microcosm.md` §12.2 item 1, §13.3).

## 2. Onset detection literature and methods

### 2.1 Taxonomy (Bello et al. 2005 — the field's standard reference)

Bello, Daudet, Abdallah, Duxbury, Davies & Sandler's "A Tutorial on Onset Detection in Music Signals" (IEEE TSAP 13(5), 2005) is the most-cited framework in the field and structures every later paper's comparisons, including Dixon 2006 (§2.2) and the aubio/Essentia implementations (§7). Its reduction methods, in increasing sophistication:

| Class | Mechanism | Verified characterization (Bello et al.) |
|---|---|---|
| **Amplitude envelope** | Rectify + smooth (low-pass) the waveform; optionally its time-derivative, or the log-energy derivative (psychoacoustically motivated — loudness is perceived logarithmically) | "Yields satisfactory results for certain applications where strong percussive transients exist against a quiet background" — i.e. the case Brainscape's own guitar-with-fuzz and pad cases are *not*. |
| **High-Frequency Content (HFC)** | Spectrum weighted linearly by bin frequency, then summed (Masri) | "Produces sharp peaks during attack transients and is notably successful when faced with percussive onsets, where transients are well modeled as bursts of white noise." Weak on soft/tonal onsets. |
| **Spectral difference / spectral flux** | An $L^1$ or $L^2$ "distance" between successive magnitude spectra, typically half-wave rectified to count only *increases* | General-purpose, moderate cost, "the simplest and fastest algorithm" per Dixon's later re-evaluation (§2.2). |
| **Phase deviation** | Second difference of unwrapped phase per bin, or the spread of the phase-deviation distribution across bins | "Successful at detecting low- and high-frequency tonal changes regardless of their intensity" — i.e. specifically targets Brainscape's slow-attack/soft-onset case — but "suffers from variations introduced by the phases of noisy low-energy components, and from phase distortions common to complex commercial music recordings (e.g., audio effects)" — i.e. specifically undermined by Brainscape's distortion-pedal case. |
| **Complex domain** | Euclidean distance in the complex plane between the observed STFT bin and a bin *predicted* from the previous two frames assuming constant magnitude and constant phase-increment | Combines magnitude and phase information; a "good choice in general, at the cost of a slight increase in computational complexity" (Dixon 2006's summary verdict). |
| **Probabilistic/statistical models** (change-point detection, ICA "surprise" signals) | Model-fitting per frame, likelihood-ratio thresholding | Best raw accuracy in Bello's own tests (negative log-likelihood: 90.6% TP / 4.7% FP, the top result in Table I) but requires an offline training pass on representative data — impractical for a pedal that must work out-of-the-box on an unknown player's unknown rig. |
| **Time-frequency/wavelet** | Dyadic wavelet decomposition, cross-scale correlation of large coefficients | Best precise time-localization, but "performing poorly with respect to the other methods" outside simple percussive material. |

[Bello et al. 2005, full text](https://hajim.rochester.edu/ece/sites/zduan/teaching/ece472/reading/Bello_2005.pdf)

### 2.2 Head-to-head results and the standard peak-picker

Bello's own cross-database comparison (1065 hand-labeled onsets across bowed/pitched-percussive/nonpitched-percussive/complex-mixture categories) found:

- **HFC**: best for percussive/complex mixtures, worst for "softer onsets."
- **Phase deviation**: best for pitched sounds (percussive and nonpercussive) where tonal information carries the onset cue, "poor results for purely percussive sounds and complex mixtures (where it is affected by phase distortions...)."
- **Spectral difference**: sits between the two — "slightly below phase deviation for pitched sounds and just under-performing HFC for more percussive and complex sounds."
- Overall guidance offered: *"If the signal is very percussive... time-domain methods are usually adequate. ...spectral methods such as those based on phase distributions and spectral difference perform relatively well on strongly pitched transients. The complex-domain spectral difference seems to be a good choice in general, at the cost of a slight increase in computational complexity."*

Dixon's 2006 DAFx re-evaluation ("Onset Detection Revisited"), run on a much larger corpus (106,054 onsets across 13 complete Mozart piano sonatas), refined the picture and — critically for Brainscape — converged on: **spectral flux, weighted phase deviation, and complex-domain methods "all achieve a similarly high level of performance,"** with no statistically meaningful winner among the top three, and **"spectral flux has the advantage of being the simplest and fastest algorithm."** [Dixon 2006, DAFx](https://www.ofai.at/papers/oefai-tr-2006-12.pdf)

Both papers use essentially the same **peak-picking / adaptive-threshold algorithm**, which is worth reproducing exactly since it is the field's converged-on standard and directly answers §5 of this document. After normalizing the detection function $f(n)$ to zero mean / unit variance, a peak at frame $n$ is accepted as an onset iff **all three** hold:

$$f(n) \ge f(k) \ \text{for all } k \in [n-w,\ n+w] \quad \text{(local maximum, } w=3\text{)}$$
$$f(n) \ge \left(\frac{1}{mw+w+1}\sum_{k=n-mw}^{n+w} f(k)\right) + \delta \quad \text{(exceeds a wider moving-mean baseline by margin } \delta\text{, } m=3\text{)}$$
$$f(n) \ge g(n-1), \quad g(n) = \max\big(f(n),\ \alpha\, g(n-1) + (1-\alpha) f(n)\big) \quad \text{(exceeds a decaying floor)}$$

Dixon reports the third condition's contribution is "marginal... assuming a suitable value for $\delta$ is chosen" — i.e., **the moving-mean-plus-margin threshold alone (condition 2) does most of the work**, which is good news for a cheap embedded implementation: a running mean and a running local max, both O(1) per sample to update incrementally, is all the adaptive-threshold machinery this class of algorithm actually needs. [Dixon 2006, §2.6–2.7]

### 2.3 The distortion-specific failure mode, and why it isn't just a tuning problem

The clearest documented statement of *why* magnitude-based detection functions specifically struggle on material like heavily-distorted guitar comes from guitar-focused onset-detection research: a proposed spectral-sparsity alternative to standard detection functions was motivated by the finding that **standard methods' "performance may drop when comparing successive notes sharing a considerable number of harmonics or with repeated notes having insufficient increase in magnitude at their onset."** [Springer/JASMP guitar onset paper, 2021 — verified via search-indexed abstract; the publisher gated full-text access during this research, see Open Questions] This is precisely what heavy clipping does to a guitar signal: it is a broadband harmonic generator, so *every* note, regardless of pitch, acquires a dense, largely-overlapping harmonic series, and the frame-to-frame magnitude spectrum genuinely stops changing much at note boundaries. No threshold adjustment recovers information a detection function is structurally blind to — this is why the honest answer to "does spectral flux fix the distorted-guitar problem" is **partially and unreliably**, not **yes**.

A separate, corroborating and more encouraging data point from the same search: **"of the onset detection functions, spectral flux offers the best detection results"** specifically in guitar-transcription literature, alongside the caveat that a *weighted* variant of spectral flux is needed to reliably reject vibrato/tremolo-induced false peaks — i.e., spectral flux is the field's practical default for guitar specifically, but arrives with its own false-positive problem on sustained, modulated notes (a real risk for Brainscape given how often ambient/granular players lean on vibrato and slow bends).

### 2.4 The case for complex-domain / phase-deviation methods specifically for Brainscape's target failure

The mechanism worth Brainscape building around, because it is the one piece of literature that speaks directly and structurally to "heavily distorted guitar with a near-constant envelope": a complex-domain or phase-deviation detector predicts each spectral bin's next value assuming *the note currently sounding continues unchanged* (constant magnitude, constant phase increment), and flags a large deviation from that prediction as an onset. **A freshly re-plucked string — even at the identical pitch, loudness, and distortion setting as the previous note — does not continue the previous note's phase; the new pluck imposes a new, essentially uncorrelated starting phase on the string's oscillation.** This is a genuinely different physical event from "the spectrum got a bit louder" or "the spectrum's harmonic mix shifted slightly," and it is specifically why the complex-domain method was introduced: **"this ability is especially useful to detect repeatedly played notes with the same frequency and short durations,"** because "the phase deviation function makes use of the fact that frequency components of the new notes are unlikely to be in phase with the previous note." [Bello/Duxbury/Davies/Sandler, IEEE SPL 2004, "On the Use of Phase and Energy for Musical Onset Detection in the Complex Domain"]

The honest counter-finding, already noted in §2.1: phase-based methods are *themselves* degraded by "phase distortions common to complex commercial music recordings (e.g., audio effects)" — and a fuzz/distortion pedal is, definitionally, an audio effect that nonlinearly reshapes phase relationships between harmonics. **The two effects (distortion helps the phase-reset cue survive; distortion hurts the phase-spectrum's cleanliness) are not the same effect and do not simply cancel — no source found in this research quantifies the net outcome specifically for guitar distortion**, which is this document's most important open question (see Open Questions #1) and should be resolved by listening tests on Brainscape's actual candidate distortion/fuzz reference pedals before committing to a single detection function.

### 2.5 What Brainscape should conclude from the literature, stated plainly

- **No single magnitude-only method (envelope, HFC, spectral flux, or a multi-band magnitude filterbank) structurally solves the same-pitch-repeated-note-under-heavy-clipping case** — this is a phase/timing phenomenon, and only phase-aware methods (phase deviation, complex domain) have a documented mechanism for it.
- **No method solves the true-zero-transient slow-attack-pad case except by falling back to a non-onset cue** — pitch change, MIDI note-on, clock, footswitch, or (for a hex/pickup-per-string input) a dedicated envelope-crossing detector tuned per string, none of which this document's "onset detection" scope actually is.
- **Complex-domain and spectral-flux methods are, per two independent large-scale evaluations spanning 15 years (Bello 2005, Dixon 2006), statistically indistinguishable in aggregate accuracy, with spectral flux cheaper and simpler.** Absent a distortion-specific listening test, **spectral flux (optionally upgraded to complex-domain if the listening test in Open Questions #1 favors it) is the literature-justified default**, not because it is claimed to fix the failure cases Brainscape wants to market against, but because it is the best-evidenced, cheapest, general-purpose choice — the failure cases are then addressed honestly via adaptive thresholding (§5) and guaranteed non-audio fallback trigger sources (§6), not oversold as "fixed" by algorithm choice alone.

## 3. Cost on Cortex-M7 / CMSIS-DSP

### 3.1 FFT mechanics and RAM — verified from CMSIS-DSP source

`arm_rfft_fast_f32` (real FFT, float32, the natural choice for a spectral-flux/complex-domain detector) supports lengths **32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096**, dispatched from `arm_rfft_fast_init_f32()` to a size-specific initializer (e.g. `arm_rfft_fast_init_512_f32`). [CMSIS-DSP `arm_rfft_fast_init_f32.c`](https://github.com/ARM-software/CMSIS-DSP/blob/main/Source/TransformFunctions/arm_rfft_fast_init_f32.c)

**Twiddle tables are compile-time `const` data, not runtime RAM allocations** — verified directly from both the init source (`S->pTwiddleRFFT = (float32_t *) twiddleCoef_rfft_512;`, a cast pointer to an existing constant array) and the instance struct definitions:

```c
// arm_cfft_instance_f32 (non-NEON) — every field is a scalar or a pointer to const data
typedef struct {
  uint16_t fftLen;
  const float32_t *pTwiddle;
  const uint16_t  *pBitRevTable;
  uint16_t         bitRevLength;
  const uint32_t  *rearranged_twiddle_tab_stride1_arr; /* + stride2, stride3 variants */
  const float32_t *rearranged_twiddle_stride1;          /* + stride2, stride3 variants */
} arm_cfft_instance_f32;

// arm_rfft_fast_instance_f32 (non-NEON) wraps the above
typedef struct {
  arm_cfft_instance_f32 Sint;
  uint16_t               fftLenRFFT;
  const float32_t       *pTwiddleRFFT;
} arm_rfft_fast_instance_f32;
```
[CMSIS-DSP `Include/dsp/transform_functions.h`](https://github.com/ARM-software/CMSIS-DSP/blob/main/Include/dsp/transform_functions.h)

**This resolves the RAM half of the task brief's question directly: an FFT instance itself costs on the order of 32–64 bytes of RAM (scalars and pointers), and the twiddle tables it points to are compiled into flash, not RAM.** On the Daisy's memory map this is essentially free either way — the twiddle table for a 512-point real FFT is a few KB of flash, trivial against the Seed's 128 KB internal flash or (if firmware executes from QSPI, as `daisy-seed-platform.md` documents as the standard path for feature-rich pedals) the 8 MB QSPI chip.

**The actual RAM the detector needs is its working buffers**, which this document sizes for the recommended 512-window/256-hop configuration (§3.3):

| Buffer | Size (float32) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Input frame (windowed time-domain) | 512 × 4 B = 2 KiB | Overwritten each hop; can double as the Hann/Tukey-windowed copy of the last 512 samples pulled from the grain engine's own SDRAM history buffer (§3.4) |
| FFT output (packed complex spectrum) | 512 × 4 B = 2 KiB | `arm_rfft_fast_f32`'s in-place-capable output format |
| Magnitude spectrum, current frame | 257 × 4 B ≈ 1 KiB | N/2+1 bins |
| Magnitude spectrum, previous frame | 257 × 4 B ≈ 1 KiB | For the flux difference; ping-pong with the above |
| Window LUT (Hann or Tukey) | 512 × 4 B = 2 KiB, or reuse the grain engine's own 4096-entry window LUT from `grain-delay-theory.md` §3.4 at negligible extra cost | Can be shared with the granular voice engine's existing window table if sizes are made compatible |
| Detection-function history (for the moving-mean/local-max peak-picker, §2.2) | ~16–32 samples × 4 B ≈ 128 B | $w=3$, $mw=9$ per Dixon's parameters; a small ring buffer |
| **Total** | **≈ 6–8 KiB** | Comfortably inside DTCM (128 KB) or AXI SRAM (512 KB, ~480 KB usable per `post-fx-chain-looper-and-system-budget.md` §1.2) alongside the reverb tank and grain-engine accumulator already budgeted there |

### 3.2 CPU cost — derived from the standard FFT flop-count rule, cross-checked against a published reference figure

The classic, widely-cited rule of thumb for a radix-2 FFT is **fewer than $5N\log_2 N$ floating-point operations** for a complex FFT of length $N$ [CCRMA/dsprelated, "Radix 2 FFT Complexity is N Log N"](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/dft/Radix_2_FFT_Complexity.html); a real FFT of length $N$ is implemented as a complex FFT of length $N/2$ plus an $O(N)$ real/complex recombination pass, so its cost is a little more than half that of a same-$N$ complex FFT.

**Cross-check against a published figure:** ARM's own Cortex-M4/M7 DSP capabilities white paper states an expected cost of **~55,538 cycles for a 1024-point real FFT (F32) on Cortex-M4** (cited secondhand via a forum thread that quotes the white paper directly — the PDF itself did not render as extractable text during this research; treat this specific number as **reported, not independently re-verified**). This is consistent in order of magnitude with the $5N\log_2 N$ rule ($5 \times 1024 \times 10 = 51{,}200$ for a same-size complex FFT; a real FFT via the half-size-complex-FFT route plus recombination lands in a similar range). **A community-reported real-world measurement on a different Cortex-M4 part (TI MSP432, TI's own compiler) found 262,823 cycles for the same 1024-point F32 RFFT — a 4.7× gap from ARM's own expected figure** — which this document treats as evidence that **toolchain/compiler choice materially affects CMSIS-DSP's realized performance**, not as evidence that ARM's own figure is wrong; Brainscape's GCC-based Daisy toolchain should be benchmarked directly once code exists rather than assumed to match either number. [Keil forum, "CMSIS DSP RFFT - too much cycles"](https://community.arm.com/support-forums/f/keil-forum/35354/cmsis-dsp-rfft---too-much-cycles)

**Scaling to Brainscape's recommended, much smaller, real-time-appropriate window size (§3.3):** for $N=512$ (half the FFT size ARM's figure covers), the $5N\log_2 N$ rule gives roughly **half** the flop count of the 1024-point case ($5\times512\times9=23{,}040$ vs. $51{,}200$) — call it **~25,000–30,000 cycles for the FFT itself** on Cortex-M7 (assumed capable of close to one single-precision FMA per cycle for this workload; note the practical caution from a Cortex-M7 community benchmark that CMSIS-DSP's float32 kernels realize only a modest ~1.3× speed-up over naive C for at least one function class (`arm_conv_f32`, ~76,000 vs. ~107,000 cycles for a 320-sample/29-tap convolution), because **the Cortex-M4/M7 SIMD DSP extensions primarily accelerate fixed-point (int16/int32) arithmetic, not the scalar single-precision FPU pipeline** — a real, source-independent reason not to assume CMSIS-DSP's float32 FFT will hit its most optimistic per-cycle throughput. [Toradex Community, "Cortex M7 - CMSIS-DSP performance"](https://community.toradex.com/t/cortex-m7-cmsis-dsp-performance/21840)

Adding windowing (512 multiplies ≈ 512–1000 cycles), magnitude computation *without* a per-bin `sqrtf` call — spectral flux and HFC both work directly on **squared magnitude** ($re^2+im^2$), avoiding 257 expensive square-root calls entirely, a small but free optimization worth calling out explicitly — (257 bins × ~4 ops ≈ 1,200 cycles), the flux/HFC difference-and-rectify pass (257 × ~3 ops ≈ 800 cycles), and the peak-picker's O(1)-per-sample running-mean/max update (negligible, amortized over the hop): **total estimated cost per analysis frame is roughly 27,000–35,000 cycles.**

**Amortized per audio sample** (dividing by the 256-sample hop, since the FFT only runs once per hop, not once per sample): **~105–140 cycles/sample**, or **~1.1–1.4% of the Daisy's 10,000-cycle/sample budget at 48 kHz**. This is dramatically cheaper than the grain engine itself (~1,280–1,600 cycles/sample per `grain-delay-theory.md` §5.3) and is not, on this evidence, a CPU-budget concern at all — a materially different conclusion from what the placeholder line item in `post-fx-chain-looper-and-system-budget.md` §6.2 implied was still an open risk.

### 3.3 Window size, hop size, and the real-time tradeoff

The literature's own analysis windows are considerably larger than what a low-latency pedal should use: Dixon's 2006 evaluation runs at **$N=2048$ (46 ms at 44.1 kHz), hop 441 samples (10 ms, 78.5% overlap)** — chosen for offline transcription accuracy on piano recordings, not real-time responsiveness. **aubio's own shipped real-time defaults are far smaller: a 512-sample window with a 256-sample hop** [aubioonset manpage](https://aubio.org/manpages/latest/aubioonset.1.html) — i.e., aubio's own engineers, building a tool explicitly meant for real-time use, chose a window 4× smaller than the academic evaluation default specifically to buy latency. **Recommendation: follow aubio's real-time precedent (512/256) rather than the literature's offline-accuracy precedent (2048/441)** — the frequency-resolution cost (512 bins gives ~94 Hz/bin at 48 kHz, adequate to resolve a guitar's fundamental and first several harmonics for anything above the open low-E) is an acceptable trade for roughly a 4× latency reduction.

### 3.4 Latency this method adds, concretely

Any window-based method cannot emit its first meaningful output before the window has filled with new material. At 512 samples / 48 kHz: **10.67 ms** minimum fill time. With 256-sample hop, subsequent frames arrive every **5.33 ms**. The peak-picker's local-maximum condition (§2.2, $w=3$) additionally requires looking 3 frames *ahead* of a candidate peak before confirming it as a true local maximum in a fully non-causal implementation — an extra **~16 ms** if implemented exactly as published, though a causal variant (only comparing against *past* frames, accepting a small accuracy cost) removes this component entirely and is the recommended choice for a performance instrument, consistent with how a musician expects to hear their own attack essentially instantly.

**Realistic total onset-to-decision latency for a causal, real-time implementation at 512/256: roughly 11–16 ms** (one hop's worth of unavoidable fill/detection delay, plus a small margin for the running-mean threshold to have enough history to be meaningful). This is a real, additive cost on top of whatever latency the grain scheduler itself imposes before a grain becomes audible (`grain-delay-theory.md` §2.2: minimum grain-delay latency is roughly one grain length) — for a very short grain (a few ms), the onset detector's own latency could plausibly *dominate* the perceived trigger-to-sound delay. **This is the single most concrete, load-bearing argument in this document for preferring the multi-band time-domain approach (§4) specifically for onset-triggered grain modes (Strum-, Arp-, Blocks-analogues) where perceived responsiveness is part of the instrument's feel**, reserving a spectral/complex-domain method for cases where its added robustness against the phase-reset/repeated-note case is worth the latency (or for a non-real-time-critical use like driving a slower modulation parameter rather than firing an individual grain).

### 3.5 A cheaper variant worth flagging: fixed-point (Q15) FFT

Because `grain-delay-theory.md` already commits the grain/history buffer to 16-bit fixed-point storage in SDRAM (§3.1, chosen specifically to halve cache-miss rate), an onset detector reading directly from that same buffer could use `arm_rfft_fast_q15`/`arm_cfft_q15` instead of the float32 path, avoiding both a format-conversion pass and taking advantage of the Cortex-M4/M7 SIMD DSP extensions' native int16 packed-arithmetic throughput — independently-reported CMSIS-DSP benchmarks found fixed-point (int16) FFTs **4–5× faster than a generic C reference implementation, versus only ~2× for float32** on the same hardware [openaudio blogspot FFT benchmark](http://openaudio.blogspot.com/2016/09/benchmarking-fft-speed.html) — implying a Q15 path could plausibly halve the ~105–140 cycles/sample estimate in §3.2 again, at the cost of CMSIS's built-in per-stage scaling (needed to prevent fixed-point overflow) slightly reducing dynamic-range precision, which is a fully acceptable trade for a *detection* function that only needs relative, not absolute, magnitude accuracy. **Recommended as a follow-on optimization once a float32 reference implementation is validated for correctness — not a v1 requirement, since the float32 cost is already trivial against budget (§3.2).**

## 4. Multi-band time-domain detector as a cheaper alternative

### 4.1 Cost model

A small filterbank of bandpass-filtered envelope followers, each with its own adaptive threshold, is the classic "cheap" onset-detection architecture, and Klapuri's 1999 "perceptual onset detector" is the field's canonical reference for it: **eight nonoverlapping frequency bands, each independently producing onset times and intensities via a psychoacoustically-motivated intensity-coding model, finally combined across bands** [Klapuri, ICASSP 1999, via Bello et al. 2005 §II]. This document costs a simplified, pedal-appropriate version:

Per band, per channel, per sample:

| Operation | Approx. cost |
|---|---|
| 2nd-order bandpass biquad (or a cascaded one-pole HP+LP pair) | ~9 float ops |
| Full-wave rectify | 1 op |
| One-pole envelope smoothing | 2 ops |
| Running-mean/adaptive-floor update (O(1) per sample, per Dixon's finding that the moving-mean term alone does most of the work, §2.2) | ~4 ops |
| Threshold compare + hysteresis state | ~2 ops |
| **Total per band per channel** | **~18 ops** |

For **6–8 bands, stereo** (12–16 band-channel instances): **~215–290 float ops/sample**, which on a dual-issue, FMA-capable Cortex-M7 plausibly retires in well under **500 cycles/sample — under 5% of budget**, cheaper than the spectral approach's own already-trivial ~105–140 cycles/sample estimate (§3.2) mainly because it avoids the FFT's $N\log N$ term entirely and pays only $O(\text{bands})$ per sample. **Neither approach is CPU-constrained on this hardware; the deciding factors are latency (§3.4, strongly favors this approach) and detection-function reliability (§4.2, does not favor this approach for Brainscape's hardest named case).**

### 4.2 What it actually buys, honestly

Splitting a broadband envelope follower into several band-limited ones buys a real, specific improvement: **a note change that shifts energy from one frequency region to another without changing total loudness becomes visible**, because each band gets its own independent envelope and its own independent adaptive floor. This covers a meaningfully broader set of real playing situations than a single broadband follower — different-register chords, pickup-position-dependent tonal shifts, and some distortion-driven harmonic redistribution — and it directly matches the architecture of Klapuri's own psychoacoustic detector and the shipped, permissively-licensed `bonk~` (§4.3).

**What it does not buy**, and this is the honest, non-oversold conclusion this research brief specifically asked for: **a multi-band filterbank is still exclusively a magnitude-domain technique.** It has no phase channel. The same-pitch, same-loudness, same-timbre, phase-reset-only repeated-note case that motivates the complex-domain/phase-deviation literature (§2.4) — arguably the single most common "onset detection silently fails" scenario for palm-muted distorted guitar — produces **no detectable change in any band's energy** under a multi-band magnitude filterbank, for exactly the same underlying reason a single broadband envelope follower misses it. **A cheaper multi-band time-domain detector does not buy "most of the robustness" of a spectral/complex-domain approach for Brainscape's specifically-named target case; it buys a different, real, and genuinely useful slice of robustness (register/timbre-shift detection) at much lower latency, which is a legitimate and well-motivated design choice, but should not be marketed or internally assumed to have solved the same-note-chugging problem.**

### 4.3 A real, permissively-licensed reference implementation: `bonk~`

Pure Data's `bonk~` object (Miller Puckette, part of Pd's own `extra/` tree, **BSD-3-Clause / "Standard Improved BSD License"**) is a directly relevant, decades-shipped, dependency-free C implementation of essentially this architecture, and — unlike aubio or Essentia (§7) — its license permits Brainscape to study *or directly adapt its code*. Verified from source [`bonk~.c`](https://github.com/pure-data/pure-data/blob/master/extra/bonk~/bonk~.c):

- **11 filter bands by default** (`#define DEFNFILTERS 11`), each a sliding bounded-Q (constant-Q-like) filter producing a real/imaginary pair per hop, from which power is computed as $re^2+im^2$ — architecturally a lightweight bank of narrowband analysis filters rather than plain IIR bandpasses, described in Puckette/Apel/Zicarelli's original paper as outperforming "the standard envelope following technique" for percussive-onset detection. [Puckette, Apel & Zicarelli, "Real-time audio analysis tools for Pd and MSP," ICMC 1998](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265414446_Real-time_audio_analysis_tools_for_Pd_and_MSP)
- **A per-band decaying "mask"** as the adaptive floor: `maskpow *= maskdecay` applied once every `masktime` frames when no attack is registered (defaults: `masktime = 4`, `maskdecay = 0.7`), tracked against a short circular history (`MASKHIST = 8`) of recent per-band maxima — functionally the same "decaying floor tracks recent loudness, current value must clear it by a margin" idea as Dixon's $g(n)$ term (§2.2), independently arrived at in a different sub-field two decades earlier.
- **Growth hysteresis, not a single fixed threshold**: an onset is declared when the ratio of current power to the masked baseline exceeds `hithresh` (default **5×**) and is considered to have *ended* only once that ratio falls back below `lothresh` (default **2.5×**) — a two-threshold (Schmitt-trigger-style) design that directly prevents the double-triggering/chatter failure mode a single fixed threshold is prone to near its crossing point, and maps directly onto the "Retrigger Cancel" concept independently converged on by electronic drum trigger modules (§5.3).

**Recommendation:** study `bonk~`'s exact filter-bank construction and mask/hysteresis parameters as a validated starting point for Brainscape's multi-band candidate — it is real prior art with 25+ years of field use, permissively licensed, and solves the double-trigger problem in a way this document had otherwise only found described piecemeal across academic peak-picking papers.

## 5. Adaptive thresholding and auto-calibration

### 5.1 Why a fixed threshold cannot work across Brainscape's target input range

A single-coil guitar's peak output is commonly on the order of tens to a few hundred millivolts; a fuzz pedal's output can be a volt or more and is heavily compressed; a line-level synth can be anywhere from −10 dBV to +4 dBu and, per `microcosm.md`'s own documentation of the Microcosm's own instrument/line input-mode distinction, this level difference alone is large enough that Hologram ships a dedicated input-level mode switch (§2.1 of that document) purely to manage headroom — before onset detection even enters the picture. Any fixed absolute threshold on a raw detection-function value will be simultaneously too sensitive for a hot fuzz signal (triggering on residual noise/hum) and too insensitive for a quiet single-coil's pick attack. This is exactly the problem Bello et al. describe generically for music signals with "significant loudness changes... a fixed threshold will tend to miss onsets in the most quiet passages, while over-detecting during the loud ones" (§2.2) — Brainscape's problem is the same phenomenon, but across *sources* rather than across passages of one recording, which is if anything a harder version of the same problem since it can't be solved by within-signal adaptation alone; it needs an explicit calibration step.

### 5.2 Adaptive whitening — normalizing away input-level and per-source spectral-tilt differences

Stowell & Plumbley's adaptive whitening technique (ICMC 2007), shipped as a built-in preprocessing option in aubio, is the standard mechanism for exactly this problem: each spectral bin's magnitude is normalized against a **slowly-decaying memory of that bin's own recent peak value** before the detection function (HFC, spectral flux, whatever is chosen) is computed from it. The effect is that the detection function's *shape* — where its peaks are, relative to its own recent history — becomes far less dependent on the absolute input level or on a source's overall spectral tilt (a bright single-coil vs. a dark, mid-scooped fuzz), because every bin has already been rescaled against its own recent dynamic range before any cross-frame comparison happens. This is a cheap addition (one compare-and-decay-or-update, one multiply, per bin, per frame — call it ~4 extra ops × 257 bins ≈ 1,000 cycles per frame, negligible against the ~30,000-cycle FFT-frame budget in §3.2) and is a stronger, better-evidenced mechanism for cross-instrument robustness than tuning the peak-picker's threshold parameter alone. [aubio adaptive-whitening docs](https://aubio.org/manual/latest/py_spectral.html)

**Recommendation: implement adaptive whitening ahead of whichever detection function is chosen, spectral or (in a band-power sense) multi-band time-domain** — the same peak-memory-normalization idea applies equally to a filterbank's per-band envelope, effectively folding the drum-trigger world's "Sensitivity" curve (§5.3) and the spectral world's "adaptive whitening" into the same underlying mechanism: normalize against recent per-channel/per-bin dynamic range before deciding what counts as a change.

### 5.3 A concrete calibration-routine design, synthesizing the precedents found

Three real, shipped precedents converge on the same shape of solution:

- **Hologram Chroma Console**: enter a calibration menu (hold both footswitches), play normally for a few seconds, the pedal auto-selects 1 of 4 discrete input-headroom levels, remembered across power cycles and applied globally to all presets, with a manual override. [Chroma Console manual](https://www.hologramelectronics.com/pages/chroma-console-manual)
- **Roland GR-55 / GK-3**: pickup sensitivity must be calibrated **per string** — a precedent for calibrating a trigger detector against per-channel or per-source characteristics rather than assuming one global setting suffices, relevant if Brainscape ever supports a hex/multi-channel input. [Roland GR-55 pickup-sensitivity support article](https://support.roland.com/hc/en-us/articles/201919469-GR-55-Setting-the-Pickup-Sensitivity)
- **Electronic drum trigger modules** (Roland, Alesis): expose **Threshold**, **Sensitivity**, **Crosstalk/Mask**, and **Retrigger Cancel** as four separate, well-understood parameters rather than one "sensitivity" knob — a more complete and more honestly-scoped vocabulary than most guitar-pedal UIs use. [digitalDrummer, "Understanding trigger parameters"](https://digitaldrummermag.com/2023/08/10/understanding-trigger-parameters/); [Alesis DM10 manual](https://www.alesis.com/rscdn/908/documents/dm10_module___operator__s_manual___revc.pdf)

**Recommended Brainscape calibration routine, combining all three:**

1. **Entry gesture**: a footswitch-hold combination (following the Chroma Console's precedent — a discoverable, no-menu-diving gesture), with a distinct LED animation (color/pattern reserved specifically for this mode, matching Brainscape's general LED-feedback philosophy inherited from the Microcosm's panel design per `microcosm.md` §2.1).
2. **"Play normally for a few seconds"** — no metronome, no specific pattern required, matching the Chroma Console's low-friction UX. Internally: run adaptive whitening (§5.2) and accumulate (a) the noise floor (lowest-percentile RMS observed, for the silence-gate threshold, directly analogous to aubio's `-90 dB` default silence threshold, §7.1), (b) the typical attack magnitude (a percentile of detection-function peak heights observed during the calibration window, seeding the moving-mean threshold's initial margin $\delta$), and (c) — if a multi-band detector is used — which bands actually carried energy for this source, so bands that are structurally silent for e.g. a bass-heavy synth don't contribute spurious/idle false triggers.
3. **Save as a single global calibration profile**, remembered across power cycles and applied to all presets, exactly matching the Chroma Console's precedent — this is a deliberate simplification versus per-preset calibration, justified by the same reasoning Hologram already validated in production: the input source (guitar + pedals in front, or synth) rarely changes mid-session, so a global setting is the right default, with `microcosm.md`'s own already-recommended per-preset override architecture (§13.1's macro-mapping-as-data philosophy) available for a power user who genuinely wants per-preset trigger tuning later.
4. **Manual override always available**: a Sensitivity control (coarse) plus, in the editor/VST tier only (per `microcosm.md`'s two-tier parameter philosophy, §13.1), the underlying threshold/hysteresis/whitening-rate parameters directly — the pedal's front panel should never require the player to understand $\delta$, $\alpha$, or `hithresh`/`lothresh` by name, only "Sensitivity."
5. **A visible, continuous confidence/margin indicator during calibration** (not just pass/fail) — e.g., an LED brightness or color sweep showing how cleanly the calibration window separated "attack" from "noise floor" — so a player calibrating against an unusually difficult source (heavy fuzz, a very compressed pad) gets an honest signal that the pedal's confidence is lower, rather than a false "all good" result that only surfaces as mysterious missed triggers later during performance.

## 6. User-facing design

Per `microcosm.md` §13.3's own recommendation (which this document was commissioned to validate and cost), the trigger layer should expose, at minimum:

### 6.1 Adjustable sensitivity

A single front-panel Sensitivity control is the right *default-visible* surface (matching the Microcosm's macro-first panel philosophy, `microcosm.md` §13.1), internally mapped to whichever combination of threshold margin ($\delta$), hysteresis ratio (`hithresh`/`lothresh`), and adaptive-whitening decay rate the chosen detection function actually uses — the drum-trigger world's four-parameter vocabulary (§5.3) is the right *underlying* model, exposed progressively (Sensitivity on the pedal; full parameter tree in the editor/VST, per the two-tier philosophy).

### 6.2 A visible trigger indicator

The single most requested, cheapest fix implied by the Microcosm's own community criticism (`microcosm.md` §12.1: onset detection failing *silently*, with no feedback to the player about why) is a **dedicated LED that flashes on every accepted trigger event**, independent of whatever the effect itself is doing visually. This turns "the pedal isn't doing anything" (an undiagnosable failure) into "the trigger light isn't flashing" (an immediately actionable one — raise Sensitivity, recalibrate, or switch trigger source). This is a near-zero-cost addition (one GPIO/LED write per accepted onset) with outsized UX value, directly informed by the same instinct behind every drum-trigger module's per-pad "hit" LED (§5.3) and behind Doepfer/Eurorack envelope-follower-plus-comparator modules' gate-output LEDs (§6.3).

### 6.3 Selectable trigger source per mode — the guaranteed-working fallback

`microcosm.md` §13.3 already specifies the target list: **onset (audio) / clock / stochastic / MIDI note / manual footswitch / external audio sidechain.** This document's contribution is confirming each is cheap and has real precedent:

- **Onset (audio)**: this document, §2–§5.
- **Clock / stochastic**: already the grain scheduler's other two trigger mechanisms per `grain-delay-theory.md` §3.2 (`seed = seed_probabilistic || seed_deterministic || seed_trigger`) — the audio-onset detector output (§2–§5) is simply a fourth OR'd input into that same scheduler, architecturally free.
- **MIDI note**: standard MIDI note-on handling, already required elsewhere in Brainscape's MIDI implementation (`microcosm.md` §13.7's recommendation to do MIDI "properly"); trivial to route into the same scheduler trigger input.
- **Manual footswitch**: already an existing hardware input; same routing.
- **External audio sidechain**: functionally identical to the onset detector itself, run against a second input signal rather than the main instrument input — zero additional algorithm needed, only an input-source mux ahead of whichever detector (§2 or §4) is implemented. Real hardware precedent for exactly this operation (turn an arbitrary external audio signal into a gate/trigger) is well-established in the Eurorack world: the **Doepfer A-119** ("reads the signal level at the input and produces an envelope... the comparator section generates a gate signal whenever the audio input crosses the threshold") and **Mutable Instruments Ears** (a compact input module "with a built-in envelope follower") are both dedicated modules for this single function, confirming it is both a real, wanted operation and a cheap one (a single envelope-follower-plus-comparator channel, a small fraction of even the multi-band cost model in §4.1). [Doepfer A-119](https://www.sweetwater.com/store/detail/A-119-VE--doepfer-a-119-external-input-envelope-follower-eurorack-module-vintage-edition)

**Recommendation: implement trigger source as a per-mode enum/mux feeding one common scheduler input, not as a special case wired into any one mode** — this is the direct DSP-architecture analogue of `microcosm.md` §13.2's "modes as data, not code" recommendation, and costs nothing beyond the mux itself once any one trigger source (audio onset or otherwise) is implemented.

## Recommendations for Brainscape

1. **Do not market or design around any single detection function "solving" both named failure cases.** The literature is explicit that magnitude-only methods miss soft/bowed-style onsets and that phase-based methods are themselves degraded by the exact kind of processing (distortion) Brainscape's other target failure case runs through. Design honestly around combination + fallback (recs #2–#5), not a silver-bullet algorithm, and say so in marketing copy rather than repeating the Microcosm's own overreach.
2. **Ship spectral flux (via CMSIS-DSP `arm_rfft_fast_f32`, 512-window/256-hop, following aubio's own real-time-tuned defaults) as the v1 audio-onset detector.** It is the best-evidenced, cheapest, general-purpose choice per two independent large-scale evaluations (§2.2), costs an estimated ~105–140 cycles/sample (§3.2) — under 1.5% of budget — and needs only ~6–8 KiB of RAM (§3.1), none of it consumed by twiddle tables (those are flash-resident, verified from source).
3. **Run a distortion-specific listening test before finalizing between spectral-flux and complex-domain as the shipped default** (Open Questions #1) — the theoretical case for complex-domain specifically targeting Brainscape's palm-muted/repeated-note distorted-guitar failure mode (§2.4) is strong, but the theoretical case for distortion also degrading phase-based methods (§2.1) is equally real, and no source found in this research quantifies which effect wins for guitar-style clipping specifically. Complex-domain costs only "a slight increase in computational complexity" over spectral flux per Dixon's own characterization, so the upgrade path is cheap if the listening test favors it.
4. **Implement adaptive whitening (§5.2) ahead of the detection function, unconditionally** — it is cheap (~1,000 cycles/frame, negligible), well-evidenced (Stowell & Plumbley 2007, already validated in production inside aubio), and is the mechanism that actually delivers "works across single-coil, fuzz, and line-level synth without user tuning," more so than threshold choice alone.
5. **Design the calibration routine per §5.3: a footswitch-hold gesture, "play normally for a few seconds," auto-derived noise floor + threshold margin, saved as one global profile, with a visible confidence indicator** — directly modeled on the Chroma Console's own shipped, one-gesture calibration UX (which the Microcosm conspicuously lacks, `microcosm.md` §13.3), extended to cover the trigger threshold, not just input headroom.
6. **Build the multi-band time-domain filterbank (§4) as a genuinely different, lower-latency trigger option, not as a cost-saving substitute for the spectral detector** — study `bonk~`'s 11-band/mask-decay/growth-hysteresis architecture (§4.3, BSD-licensed, directly adaptable) as the reference implementation. Position it specifically for onset-triggered grain modes where perceived responsiveness matters most (Strum/Arp/Blocks-analogues, per `microcosm.md` §4.5's reading of which Microcosm effects are onset-driven), where its ~11–16 ms latency advantage over the FFT-based path (§3.4) is most audible.
7. **Implement a visible per-onset trigger LED, independent of the effect's own visual feedback, as a near-zero-cost fix for the Microcosm's single biggest documented complaint** (silent, undiagnosable trigger failure) — one GPIO write per accepted event.
8. **Implement trigger source as a per-mode enum feeding one common scheduler input** (onset / clock / stochastic / MIDI note / footswitch / external sidechain), architecturally free once the scheduler's existing OR'd trigger inputs (`grain-delay-theory.md` §3.2) are extended by one more source — this delivers `microcosm.md` §13.3's "guaranteed-working fallback" at essentially no additional engineering cost.
9. **Write the onset-detection layer from scratch — DaisySP has no envelope-follower, gate, or onset primitive to build on** (verified absence from `daisysp.h`), the same situation Brainscape already faces for the grain engine (`grain-delay-theory.md` §4.3) and the looper (`post-fx-chain-looper-and-system-budget.md` §4.1) — plan the engineering effort accordingly rather than assuming a library shortcut exists.
10. **Reserve the Q15/fixed-point FFT path (§3.5) as a follow-on optimization, not a v1 requirement** — the float32 path is already comfortably inside budget; only revisit this if real hardware measurement (rec #11) shows otherwise.
11. **Measure, don't just estimate, once real code exists** — every cycle figure in §3 is derived from published/secondhand reference numbers and the standard FFT flop-count rule, explicitly not benchmarked on Brainscape's own Daisy toolchain (consistent with the epistemic standard the sibling documents already hold themselves to). Put a DWT cycle counter around the real implementation before treating any number in this document as final, especially given the documented 4.7× toolchain-dependent variance found in §3.2.

## Open questions

1. **Whether complex-domain/phase-deviation methods net-outperform plain spectral flux specifically on heavily distorted/fuzz-pedal guitar was not resolved by any source found in this research.** §2.4 identifies two real, documented, opposing effects (distortion helps the phase-reset cue survive repeated-same-note playing; distortion's own nonlinear reshaping of phase relationships hurts phase-based detection generally) but no source quantifies the net outcome for this specific signal class. **This should be resolved empirically** — record a small reference set (clean DI guitar, several distortion/fuzz pedals at increasing gain, palm-muted single-note chugging, sustained pad-like bends) and run both a spectral-flux and a complex-domain implementation against it before committing to one as Brainscape's shipped default.
2. **The guitar-specific spectral-sparsity paper (Springer/JASMP 2021) could not be read in full** — the publisher's site redirected to an authentication wall during this research, and its findings above are drawn from a search-indexed abstract/summary, not the full paper. Its proposed alternative detection function (spectral sparsity) is not evaluated in this document at all as a result, and might be worth a dedicated follow-up read if library/institutional access is available.
3. **Stowell & Plumbley's adaptive-whitening paper's exact algorithmic parameters** (the peak-memory decay rate, specifically) **could not be independently verified from the primary source** — a direct PDF fetch failed a TLS certificate validation error during this research, and this document's description of the technique is drawn from aubio's own documentation and secondary summaries, not the original paper's equations. Worth a follow-up fetch via a different network path or a university mirror before finalizing the exact decay-rate constant to ship.
4. **No hardware measurement exists for either candidate detector on Daisy Seed hardware or even on any Cortex-M7 part specifically for an FFT this small (512-point).** Every cycle estimate in §3 is derived from a mix of the standard FFT flop-count rule, one secondhand ARM white-paper figure (for a different, larger FFT size, on Cortex-M4), and one community-reported real-world measurement that came in 4.7× higher than that figure on a different toolchain. The actual number on Brainscape's GCC/Daisy toolchain, once code exists, should supersede everything in §3.2.
5. **Whether Cortex-M7's dual-issue superscalar pipeline meaningfully reduces CMSIS-DSP's float32 FFT cycle count versus the Cortex-M4 figures cited throughout §3.2 was not established** — the one M7-specific data point found (Toradex community, `arm_conv_f32`) suggests CMSIS-DSP's float32 kernels don't benefit much from the SIMD DSP extensions regardless of M4 vs. M7, but this is a single data point for a different function (convolution, not FFT) and should not be treated as conclusive for the FFT case specifically.
6. **This document did not evaluate onset detection cost or reliability for a genuinely polyphonic/multi-string input** (e.g., a future hex-pickup or multi-channel Brainscape variant) — every method and cost figure above assumes a single summed audio channel, consistent with the pedal's currently-planned mono-in/stereo-out or stereo-in I/O per `pedal-control-surface-and-io-hardware.md`, not a per-string signal path. Roland's per-string GR-55 calibration precedent (§5.3) was included as forward-looking context, not as evidence this document actually costed a per-string implementation.
7. **No listening/perceptual test exists (nor could one be conducted through documentation review alone) to confirm the §3.4 latency estimate (11–16 ms) is actually imperceptible or acceptable in the specific context of a fast onset-triggered grain mode** — the estimate is a sum of well-sourced component delays (window fill, hop quantization, peak-picker look-ahead), not a measured or perceptually-validated end-to-end figure. Given how directly this bears on rec #6's "position the multi-band detector for responsiveness-critical modes" recommendation, a real A/B listening comparison once both detectors exist would be the natural next step.
